## Why this case

FTL is particularly useful for testing how independent developers create their production conditions. It combines:

- Professional game-development experience held by both core developers before the project.
- Personal savings providing runway after leaving employment.
- Shanghai as a distinctive development location.
- A small prototype → competition validation → commercialization → Kickstarter transition.
- A two-person core team whose members did not perform every task themselves.
- Deliberate constraints on representation and technical complexity.
- A Kickstarter outcome that can overshadow the earlier history and produce a crowdfunding myth.

The purpose is to separate these production variables, rather than establish that cheap living in Shanghai caused FTL's success.

## Provisional Timeline

### Before 2009 / education and hobby work

- Matthew Davis had a Computer Science education and subsequently entered professional game development.
- Justin Ma leaned toward art and design. In a 2013 interview he recalled spending substantial spare time at university developing game ideas and small prototypes.

Evidence status: SUPPORTED (P1 near-contemporary interview).

### 2009 — 2K Shanghai

- Justin Ma joined 2K Shanghai in 2009 in game design.
- Matthew Davis also worked there as a programmer.
- They met in Shanghai in a large, conventional studio environment.

Evidence status: SUPPORTED.

### 2011 — prototype / transition out of studio work

- Multiple sources agree that FTL did not begin with a definite commercial plan to quit, found a company and become the next successful indie studio.
- Contemporary and retrospective interviews describe a small project, hobby project or prototype, including a project between jobs to keep creating and expand their résumés.
- A contemporary 2012 interview explicitly states that they had worked in traditional studios and **saved enough money to give themselves a few months to make a small project together**.

Retrospective accounts compress the timing of departure, brainstorming and prototyping differently. A single precise date is not yet asserted.

### 2011 — China Independent Games Festival

- FTL was a China Independent Games Festival 2011 finalist.
- This occurred before Kickstarter.
- Later recollections emphasize meeting other developers and receiving serious feedback at China IGF as an important signal that others were interested in the prototype.

Evidence status: SUPPORTED.

### Early 2012 — wider validation before crowdfunding

- FTL received honorable mention recognition including Excellence in Design at IGF 2012.
- The developers also later described early media attention, such as PC Gamer, as important to recognizing potential demand.

Kickstarter therefore must not be described as FTL's **first** market validation.

### 2012-02-27 — 2012-04-01 — Kickstarter

The original Kickstarter page records:

- Location: Shanghai, China.
- Goal: **USD 10,000**.
- Pledged: **USD 200,542**.
- Backers: **9,818**.
- Duration: 33 days.

The campaign raised approximately **20 times its goal**.

> USD 200,542 is the pledged amount. It is neither the complete development budget nor net proceeds, and it does not mean FTL was financed only by this money from its first day.

### 2012 — release

- FTL completed its commercial release in 2012.
- The 2013 GDC postmortem could retrospectively describe the design path from concept through crowdfunding to release.

## Myth Audit

### Myth A — “Two people made a game, put it on Kickstarter and became an overnight success”

**PARTIALLY REFUTED / compressed narrative.**

Current evidence shows at least five layers of prior history:

1. Education and hobby prototypes.
2. Professional development experience at 2K Shanghai.
3. Personal savings providing a few months of initial runway.
4. A small project / prototype.
5. China IGF, IGF, media and peer feedback.
6. Only then, an exceptionally successful Kickstarter campaign.

Kickstarter matters, but it is not the beginning of the story.

### Myth B — “FTL is purely a two-man game”

**The definition needs clarification.**

Justin Ma and Matthew Davis clearly form the core team, but external contributions must be counted separately, including music by Ben Prunty.

Prefer **two-person core development team**, rather than imply that two people did all creative, audio, QA and publishing work.

### Myth C — “They decided from the outset to gamble their careers on a startup”

**Not currently supported.**

Davis later explicitly described the initial goal as a hobby / prototype / résumé project, with finding more interesting studio employment among the expected next steps.

> FTL's initial downside was not a binary choice between success and bankruptcy. Re-employment itself provided an implicit safety net.

This matters to C001/C002/C006.

### Complementarity

FTL is not simply the combination of two interchangeable all-round independent developers. The currently more plausible structure is:

> **A programming-heavy founder + an art/design/scripting-heavy founder + shared design + external specialist contributions.**

This complementary combination may be more useful to study than the headcount of two itself.

## Runway

### VERIFIED / supported

- The initial phase had personal savings-funded runway.
- A contemporary 2012 interview described enough time for “a few months” on a small project.
- Kickstarter provided funding far above the goal, moving a small, externally validated project toward more secure commercial completion.

### UNKNOWN

- Their specific savings amounts.
- Monthly burn after leaving employment.
- Shanghai rent and living costs.
- Partner, family or other income support.
- Net usable Kickstarter proceeds after fees, tax and project expenses.
- Opportunity cost.

> “FTL became possible because Shanghai had low living costs” currently remains H, not a Case fact.

## Production / Scope

A 2013 Game Developer interview provides concrete division-of-work and tooling clues:

- Ma: art and significant scripting.
- Davis: programming-heavy.
- Shared design.
- Photoshop.
- Game Maker for animation and small feature tests.
- Notepad++ scripting.
- SVN version control.

FTL did not pursue the cost structure of large contemporary space games. Later production-history material also states that the team lacked both the resources and intention to undertake complex 3D representation. Pixel/2D representation better matched the art skills Ma could cover at the time.

### Provisional production interpretation — H

FTL's key innovation may have been to re-represent the fantasy of captaining a starship through rooms, systems, crew, events and resource decisions, rather than 3D piloting and cinematic presentation.

That changes an expensive space-simulator problem into an information-and-systems simulation that the two developers' capabilities could support. This interpretation still needs verification through the GDC talk and prototype evolution.

## Failure / Risk

The currently important risk facts concern the initial commitment, rather than a specific near-death crisis:

- The original planned scope was small.
- The creators expected limited market appeal.
- They lacked experience in commerce and company operations.
- They increased commitment only as positive competition, media and player feedback accumulated.

> Rather than assemble resources around a huge initial vision, they let external evidence progressively increase their commitment.

## Market Path

FTL is not a simple `zero marketing / pure organic` case. The known path includes:

1. Competition submission.
2. China IGF 2011 finalist status.
3. IGF recognition.
4. Early enthusiast and media attention.
5. A Kickstarter page and campaign outreach.
6. Release platforms and storefront exposure.

The useful question is how they built credibility and market access progressively without a conventional large advertising budget.

## Environment / Luck

### Environment — supported

- They met and acquired professional experience in a large game studio in Shanghai.
- The public Kickstarter campaign location was Shanghai, China.
- China IGF provided an early external-validation point.

### Environment — still UNKNOWN

- Shanghai's actual cost advantage.
- Residence, visa and tax conditions in China.
- How much these conditions extended runway compared with similar developers in the United States.

### Luck / right-tail events

- Kickstarter's exceptional result was a strong right-tail market response.
- Competition and media attention created successive positive feedback.

Luck does not erase the earlier playable prototype or the decision to submit it to competitions.

## Provisional Verdict

The first evidence pass suggests a **staged commitment model**:

> Professional capability capital → savings buying a few months of free time → a small prototype → active exposure to competitions and peers → increased commitment following external feedback → crowdfunding expanding runway and validating demand → a completed commercial product.

This is a strong case for studying the creation of production conditions. It does not establish that:

- Everyone should quit employment.
- Large-studio experience is necessary.
- Low Shanghai costs were the central cause.
- Crowdfunding was the main cause of success.
- Small teams are inherently more innovative.

## Transferable

First-pass candidates:

- Design a small experiment with limited runway before committing to a full startup.
- Preserve re-employment and career fallback paths to reduce the permanent cost of a failed experiment.
- Combine complementary capabilities in the core team rather than prioritize low headcount alone.
- Recut expensive fantasies through representation and scope choices.
- Seek high-quality external prototype review before formal financing.
- Use real feedback to decide whether to increase commitment.

These require cross-Case validation. One FTL case cannot turn them into universal prescriptions.

## Non-transfer / Context-specific

- The timing of the 2011–2012 Kickstarter / indie boom.
- Contemporary competition in roguelike-like / PC indie markets.
- The China IGF / IGF route.
- Professional skills the two developers already possessed.
- Kickstarter's unusually strong response.
